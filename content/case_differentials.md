<div id="content"><p class="noindent">Every student studying a field related to optimization should be able to work with
<span class="cmcsc-10"><span class="small-caps">differentials</span></span>. They are simpler to apply than (partial) derivatives and strictly
more expressive, especially when generalizing to matrix functions. We will motivate
them in this post, following the book <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119541219">Matrix Differential Calculus</a>. </p>

<!-- more --> 

<p class="indent">   In university of any engineering degree, they teach you the partial derivatives
of a function. It tells you the <span class="cmcsc-10"><span class="small-caps">rate of change</span> </span>between an input and an
output of the function and uses the convention to represent them as
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">∂𝑦</mi><mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">∕</mo><mi mathvariant="italic">∂𝑥</mi></mrow></math>. That
works for functions with scalar input and scalar output, but does not generalize well for
vector/matrix calculus. Also this convention is rather weird, as it introduces a
fraction notation, while not being a real fraction (your are not allowed to move
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">∂𝑥</mi></mrow></math>
out).
</p><p class="indent">   Instead, <span class="cmcsc-10"><span class="small-caps">differentials</span> </span>use a different approach. They represent how a change of the
input <math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi></mrow></math>
should be transformed to arrive at the linear part of the output
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>y</mi></mrow></math>.
Linear, because the term is only allowed to depend on
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi></mrow></math> once, for
example for <math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi>ϕ</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <msup><mrow><mi>x</mi></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></msup></mrow></math>

                                                                  
</p><math display="block" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mtable class="align-star" columnalign="left" displaystyle="true" rowlines="" style="">
               <mtr><mtd class="align-odd" columnalign="right"><mi>ϕ</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi> <mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">+</mo> <mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <msup><mrow><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi> <mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">+</mo> <mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></msup> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <msup><mrow><mi>x</mi></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></msup> <mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">+</mo> <mn>2</mn><mi>x</mi><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi> <mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">+</mo> <msup><mrow><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></msup></mtd>               <mtd class="align-even"><mspace width="2em"></mspace></mtd>               <mtd class="align-label" columnalign="right">
   </mtd></mtr></mtable></math>
<p class="noindent">the first differential is given as <math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>ϕ</mi> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <mn>2</mn><mi>x</mi><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi></mrow></math>.
</p><p class="indent">   The function parameters may be scalar, vector or matrix, for
demonstration purpose we will concentrate on scalar functions with matrix
parameters, often encountered in machine learning context and denote them as
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi>ϕ</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>F</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math>.
</p></div>

<h3>Identification of Jacobian</h3>
<div id="content"><p class="noindent">Another way to express the relation between first-order derivatives and first-order
differentials is the following identity
</p><p class="indent">
</p><math display="block" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mtable class="align-star" columnalign="left" displaystyle="true" rowlines="" style="">
                   <mtr><mtd class="align-odd" columnalign="right"><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>f</mi> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <mi>J</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi><mspace class="thinspace" width="0.17em"></mspace><mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">⟺</mo><mspace class="thinspace" width="0.17em"></mspace><mfrac><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">∂𝑓</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow> 
  <mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">∂𝑥</mi></mrow></mfrac>   <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <mi>J</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mtd>                   <mtd class="align-even"><mspace width="2em"></mspace></mtd>                   <mtd class="align-label" columnalign="right">
   </mtd></mtr></mtable></math><p>
with the Jacobian <math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi>J</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math> describing

To rewrite a differential to a derivative, we need to order elements such that we can
express the Jacobian in the LHS relation above.                          

                                                                  
</p><p class="indent">   Comparing this to the approach of many machine learning frameworks, we see the
similarity to vector-Jacobian product (vJp) to transform a gradient vector in a chain
of Jacobian transformations. Sometimes, respresenting the transformation as a
linear map is hard, and we can represent the vJp in a more general form for
<span class="cmti-10">differentials</span>.
</p></div>

<h3>Going to Second Differentials</h3>
<div id="content"><p class="noindent">Producing more rules, we can derive Hessian from the second differential.
It is straightforward defines as taking the differential, of a differential:
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>f</mi><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>X</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math>. third differential is again defines, as taking the differential of the second differential, and
so on <math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mi>…</mi></mrow></math>.

identify the Hessian from the second differential. We formally define the

<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>ϕ</mi> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <msup><mrow><mi>J</mi></mrow><mrow><mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">⊺</mo></mrow></msup><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi></mrow></math> and
<math display="inline" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mrow><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>J</mi> <mo class="MathClass-rel" stretchy="false">=</mo> <mi>H</mi><msup><mrow><mo class="MathClass-open" stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo class="MathClass-close" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mo class="MathClass-bin" stretchy="false">⊺</mo></mrow></msup><mstyle class="text"><mtext>d</mtext></mstyle><mi>x</mi></mrow></math>, then

### Conclusion

I hope you got an idea why differentials are
a handy tool when working with optimization or Machine Learning. Personally I found them helpful many times when deriving some algorithm for machine learning.

We showcased only some calculus rules to highlight the advantage of differentials compared to derivatives. For actual applications take a look at [Chapter 11](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119541219.ch11) for inequalities and [Chapter 15](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119541219.ch15) for Maximum Likelihood Estimation. I recommend the whole book, there is also a condensed overview in the last chapter.
